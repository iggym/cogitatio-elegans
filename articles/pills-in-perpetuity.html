Pills in Perpetuity

Modern medicine isn't failing to cure chronic disease. It's succeeding at managing it — because that's what it was built to do.

The Spectrum of Medical Incentive
Cure Manage Maintain Depend

Where capital concentrates: leftward is rare and uneconomic. Rightward is where quarterly earnings live.

Counterfeit Compassion

Margaret stands at the pharmacy counter on a Tuesday in March. Seven white bags slide across the counter.

Metformin for her blood sugar. Lisinopril for her blood pressure. Atorvastatin for her cholesterol. Escitalopram for the anxiety she developed the year her husband died. Omeprazole for the reflux that started when the anxiety did. Levothyroxine for her thyroid. A low-dose aspirin because her doctor said it couldn't hurt.

"See you next month," the pharmacist says, smiling the same smile he's smiled for eleven years.

Margaret is sixty-eight. She has been on this exact regimen for eleven years. No one — not her primary care doctor, not her cardiologist, not her endocrinologist — has ever sat her down and said: here is the path off these medications.

Not because they don't care. Because the path doesn't exist in the system they were trained inside.

Reading the Receipt

The medical-industrial complex — the triad of pharmaceutical manufacturers, insurance payers, and hospital systems — is economically optimized for chronic management, not cures.

Lifelong pharmaceutical dependence generates exponentially more lifetime capital and predictable recurring revenue than a one-time curative treatment. Healthcare systems are engineered around financial models of disease maintenance, making true preventative wellness economically counter-incentivized.

This isn't a conspiracy. It's a spreadsheet.

Following the Money

Start with the arithmetic. Curing and managing aren't just different strategies — they're opposing business models.

Compare two hypotheticals from the manufacturer's side: a one-time gene therapy that permanently reverses Type 1 diabetes, priced at $500,000 — versus daily insulin for forty years at $3,000 per year, totaling $120,000.

The cure nets more per patient, once. The management nets less per year but compounds across millions of patients, every year, forever. Wall Street doesn't value the one-time sale. It values the recurring revenue stream. That's why insulin has had sixty different patent extensions and zero curative competitors.

Where Capital Goes Antibiotic research — drugs you take for ten days and stop — has been starved to the point of crisis. Of the eighteen largest pharmaceutical companies, fifteen have abandoned antibiotic R&D entirely. Meanwhile, oncology and immunology pipelines swell with drugs designed to be taken indefinitely, drugs that extend life by months rather than years, priced at six figures annually.

The system isn't broken. The system is doing exactly what it was incentivized to do.

Perspective Snap

Every cure is a cancelled subscription.

The Lifelong Ledger

This is not a claim about individual doctors.

The physician who refills Margaret's metformin is not venal. She went into medicine to heal. She refills the prescription because the system she works inside gives her eleven minutes per patient, no time for root-cause investigation, and a billing code that pays for the prescription, not the conversation that might prevent it.

Nor is this a claim that every chronic disease has a cure waiting in a vault somewhere. Type 1 diabetes is genuinely hard. Alzheimer's is genuinely hard. Autoimmune disease is genuinely hard. The point is not that cures exist and are being hidden. The point is that the economic engine of medicine has no reason to look for them aggressively — and every reason to extend the runway of management.

The system resists cures even when they arrive.

Yes, there are exceptions. Vertex Pharmaceuticals pursued a functional cure for sickle cell disease. Some biotech firms pursue one-time gene therapies. But note what happens when they succeed: the curative therapy is priced at $2.2 million per patient, insurers balk, and adoption crawls. The cure exists. The system has no idea what to do with it.

Pilot Light, Not Bonfire

You are not powerless inside this system. You are a participant who can ask different questions.

Concrete moves:

  • → For every medication you take indefinitely, ask your doctor: "What would it take to get me off this?" Not confrontationally. Curiously. Watch what happens to the conversation.
  • → When offered a new prescription, ask: "Is this treating the cause or managing the symptom?" Both are legitimate. You should know which one you're paying for.
  • → If you have a chronic condition, search clinicaltrials.gov for curative rather than management trials. They exist. They're under-enrolled because no one told patients to look.
  • → Track your own data. Blood pressure, glucose, sleep, mood. Bring patterns to your doctor. The system gives her eleven minutes — bring her the conclusion, not the puzzle.

None of this dismantles the medical-industrial complex. But each move reclaims a sliver of agency the system was designed to absorb.

Ask the Hard Question

Within the next 24 hours — one specific move.

  • Open your medicine cabinet tonight.
  • For every bottle marked "refill as needed" or "refill indefinitely," write on a sticky note: What would cure what this treats?
  • Stick it to your bathroom mirror.
  • Tomorrow, call your doctor's office and ask for fifteen minutes to discuss the answers — not to demand a cure, but to find out whether your doctor has ever asked the question herself.

That's the shift. The system is not going to ask it for you.
