On worth, in an age of plenty

Scarcity Changed Address

The machines are about to out-perform you at almost everything. Here's the one line of work they can't take — and why it's already rearranging who gets paid for what.

The Barista Test

You walk into a coffee shop with a touchscreen kiosk sitting dark in the corner, unplugged in every way that matters, while a person behind the counter asks how your week's going and gets your order right without you repeating it. That's not nostalgia. It's a business decision. Starbucks announced it was walking back its bet on self-order automation and hiring more baristas instead, because customers wanted to be served by a human being1.

Meanwhile the people who build the machines are suddenly obsessed with something that sounds almost quaint. Silicon Valley's most influential builders — Sam Altman among them, alongside Paul Graham — have started writing essays about taste. Founders are enrolling in etiquette classes. None of this is a coincidence, and none of it is really about coffee or manners. It's a live rehearsal for a question that's about to hit everyone: if a machine can out-code you, out-write you, out-diagnose you, and out-design you, what exactly are you being paid for right now?

You're the Currency

Here's the claim: when intelligence and labor stop being scarce, the thing that stays scarce is you — specifically, the fact that you're a person, in the room, paying attention. Not your résumé. Not your output. Your presence.

The economist Alex Imas made this argument in one of the sharper essays on the subject: once AI can match or beat humans at essentially every task, value doesn't vanish, it relocates — away from what you can do and toward the simple, stubborn fact of being human and being there2. That's a genuinely uncomfortable thought if your entire sense of worth is built on being good at things. It means the goalposts aren't shrinking. They're moving to a field you didn't know you were standing on.

The pessimist's version of this future already has a name: Nosedive, the 2016 Black Mirror episode where every interaction gets rated one to five stars, and your cumulative score decides your rent, your friends, your worth3. Pastel, smiling, and suffocating. If human presence really is about to become the most valuable thing in the economy, that's the fear worth sitting with: a single, universal ranking of who's worth talking to.

A Thousand Ponds

But a world that prices presence doesn't have to converge on one ranking. It's more likely to do the opposite.

Society was never one arena. It's a patchwork of smaller ones, each running on its own currency and its own hierarchy — a structure sociologists call fields. A chef doesn't lose sleep over a senator's approval. A junior academic doesn't envy a hedge fund manager's bonus. They're not competing, because they're not in the same field. And the internet already proved how far this fragmentation can go: once distribution got cheap, we ended up with whole thriving subcultures built around marble racing, competitive whistling, flag design — audiences too small to exist in any single town, big enough to exist online.

If AI does the same thing to production that the internet did to distribution — makes it nearly free to make something for an audience of five hundred — then the number of these small fields doesn't shrink. It multiplies. And inside each one, someone still becomes the person everyone else measures themselves against; superstar economics shows up at every scale, not just the biggest one4. The best fountain-pen reviewer on the internet doesn't split an audience with the tenth-best. The field is small. The hierarchy inside it is steep regardless.

You don't need to win the world.
You need to matter in one room.

Old Story, New Stakes

None of this is as new as it feels, and that's actually reassuring. Every time the basis of value has shifted, human behavior has bent to fit it — and people adapted. The factory rewarded punctuality, and it rewired how entire populations experienced time itself, a shift the historian E.P. Thompson traced from the loose rhythms of rural work to the clock-governed discipline of industrial labor5. Literacy did something similar at the level of the brain: learning to read measurably repurposes the visual cortex, recruiting territory that once specialized in recognizing faces6. Even belief systems have functioned as incentive systems — Max Weber's account of Calvinist theology helping produce the work ethic behind early capitalism7, and economists have even found that a strong cultural belief in hell correlates with better economic outcomes, presumably because the threat of eternal punishment is a remarkably effective motivator8.

Social skill being economically decisive isn't new either. Among the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer society, being well-liked is one of the two things that actually determines your status9. And even inside our current, supposedly cognition-obsessed economy, the wage premium on social skills has been rising steadily since the 1980s — including in famously awkward industries like tech10. The relational economy isn't arriving from nowhere. It's been quietly gaining ground for forty years.

It's also fair to worry this shift isn't neutral. Relational skill is itself substantially heritable — roughly as heritable as IQ, according to twin studies11 — so a relational economy won't be some pure meritocracy free of the arbitrary advantages that shaped the old one. Fair concern. But it's worth remembering the cognitive economy was never that clean either: IQ is somewhere between 50 and 80 percent heritable, and conscientiousness, the trait that best predicts job performance, sits around 60 percent12. No one earns their starting line in either economy. And relational capital has already shown it can travel outside traditional class routes — creators with no elite cultural pedigree at all have built enormous audiences purely on presence and charisma, which suggests this new currency isn't simply old money in a new coat.

Pick Your Pond

If value is fragmenting into a thousand small fields instead of collapsing into one master ranking, the practical move isn't to become generically likable, or to chase a single viral audience. It's to find — or build — the specific, smaller room where your presence already registers.

That's a decision you can actually make, today, in specific and unglamorous ways: showing up consistently to the same Tuesday run club instead of a different gym every week. Replying to people in your group chat by name and by memory, not just emoji. Choosing one niche — woodworking, distance running, a particular strain of internet humor, your kid's Little League team — and becoming a known face there instead of a stranger everywhere. Presence compounds locally. It doesn't compound by being spread thin across everyone.

Before this time tomorrow

Do this by tomorrow

Pick one room where people would actually notice if you stopped showing up — a group chat, a team, a regular table at the same coffee shop, a Sunday call with your parents. Not your biggest audience. Your realest one.

Then do one thing there in the next 24 hours that only an attentive, present human could do: remember a detail someone mentioned weeks ago and bring it up. Show up in person instead of texting. Ask a real follow-up question and actually wait for the answer.

That's the whole exercise. Not building a personal brand. Just proving, to one small field, that you're worth being in the room.
