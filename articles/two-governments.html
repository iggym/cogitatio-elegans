Two Governments, One Year

We tell the story of human politics as a straight line: bands, then chiefs, then kings, then ballots. But some of the first political scientists on Earth ran two systems at once — and switched between them on a schedule.

WINTER · CHAIN OF COMMAND SUMMER · OPEN COUNCIL
Not a progression. A pendulum — the same people, the same year, both ends on purpose.

01Before First Light

The camp wakes before the sun does. Nobody needs to be told twice.

A man walks the lodges giving orders in a voice nobody argues with. Scouts move out first, then the drivers, then the families with the hides and the knives. Every position is assigned. Anyone who breaks formation, chases early, or spooks the herd before the signal isn't just scolded — among several Plains societies, the policing societies charged with keeping order during the communal hunt had real, sanctioned force: they could confiscate property, destroy a lodge, or even kill the dogs of a person who ruined the drive.

For the length of the winter buffalo hunt, this camp is not a democracy. It is closer to a small, temporary military state, and everyone in it agreed to that, because a scattered herd means a hungry season.

02The Bold Reframe

Here's the standard story, the one most of us absorbed without ever being taught it directly: human political life evolved in one direction. Small egalitarian bands gave way to agriculture, agriculture gave way to chiefs and then states, and states — eventually, haltingly — gave way to modern democracy. Freedom is the finish line we're still running toward.

That story treats early societies as not yet arrived — proto-democracies waiting for the technology and the enlightenment to catch up.

The reframe: some pre-modern societies weren't waiting for anything. They had already built, used, and dismantled more than one political system — within a single year — because they understood something we've mostly forgotten: a system of government is a tool you pick up for a job, not an identity you grow into.

03Two Governments, One Year

Look at the seasonal rhythm of Great Plains nations and it splits cleanly in two. Winter — the communal bison hunt — called for speed, coordination, and obedience, so authority consolidated into police societies with the power to enforce it. Summer — when bands reunited for large gatherings — called for something else entirely: consensus, debate, and shared standing among many voices, and the winter enforcement structures simply dissolved. No coup. No civil war. No crisis of legitimacy. Just a seasonal changeover, understood by everyone as temporary and functional from the start.

Pacific Northwest communities show a related pattern in their own vocabulary: concentrated, almost formal authority during resource-critical periods — coordinating salmon runs, managing communal harvests — loosening into flatter, more consensus-driven arrangements once that pressure eased.

The hierarchy wasn't a stage they were passing through. It was a setting they turned on and off.
They weren't climbing a ladder toward democracy. They were switching tools in the same toolbox.

This is the hidden connection the linear story hides: authoritarian and consensus-based governance weren't rival endpoints of history, competing to be the "real" destination. In these societies, they were compatible parts of one working system, deployed on a schedule as deliberate as the hunt itself.

04What This Isn't

This isn't a claim that every pre-modern society ran this way, or that seasonal switching was universal across all Indigenous North American nations — political organization varied enormously by region, resource base, and era, and the ethnographic record is uneven. It's also fair to ask whether "self-conscious architecture" overstates what may have started as pragmatic adaptation to hunting logistics.

But the switching itself is the hard evidence against the pragmatic-accident reading. An arrangement that appears only under pressure and vanishes the moment the pressure lifts, without conflict, without renegotiation, and without anyone treating the change as remarkable, isn't an accident nobody noticed. It's a tool nobody had to think twice about picking up.

05Your Own Two Seasons

You already do a version of this, even if no one's named it for you. The team that runs open-floor brainstorms on Tuesday and locks into a strict chain of command during a launch-week fire drill isn't being inconsistent. It's running the same seasonal logic: consensus when the goal is finding the right idea, hierarchy when the goal is executing one fast, under pressure, with no room for a debate that costs you the herd.

The agency here is small and concrete. It's not "embrace flexible leadership" as a poster on a wall. It's: before the next high-stakes push, someone in the room says out loud, "we're switching to winter rules until this is done," and everyone knows exactly what that means and when it ends — because it was named, not defaulted into.

06One Thing, Today

Name your season

Pick one recurring meeting, project, or team you're part of. In the next 24 hours, decide out loud which mode it's actually in right now — and whether that's the mode it should be in.

  • Say which mode you're defaulting to: consensus or command — most groups never name it, they just drift.
  • Ask if the current mode fits the actual stakes this week, not the group's habitual style.
  • If it doesn't fit, say so and set an end date for the switch — a season, not a permanent shift.
