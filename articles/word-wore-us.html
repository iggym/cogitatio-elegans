Words Wore Us

A parasite moved into your skull before you turned two. It's still there. It's talking right now — and you've been calling the voice "your thoughts."

Orthodox View Reframed
Language is a neutral tool we invented to share what we already think. Language colonized our brains and made us rebuild the planet in its image.

01 — OpeningThe Clock Bites First

The 7:42 was three minutes gone when Mara hit the platform.

The word late arrived before the breath did — small, serrated, electric. It rewired her stomach, her gait, the angle of her shoulders. The conductor's whistle was already history. The schedule bolted to the pole, the watch on her wrist, the bell in the tower down the street, the factory shifts her grandfather lived and died by — an entire continent of iron and glass built to enforce a word.

Time. Without that word, the platform is just concrete. The 7:42 is just light moving across a sign. Mara is just a mammal in a coat, breathing.

She wasn't late. She was being spoken.

02 — The ReframeHost, Not Author

Here is the claim, plainly stated.

We did not invent language. Language domesticated us, then used our nervous systems to redesign the planet into a place more hospitable to itself.

Every word is a packet of instructions. Most expire inside a single conversation. A few — time, property, sin, money, nation — turn out to be exquisitely good at rewriting the brains that carry them, so those brains rearrange the world to match.

Clocks. Fences. Prayers. Banks. Borders. These are not inventions of humans. They are outcroppings of vocabulary — physical structures forced into being by abstract constructs that needed somewhere stable to live.

Lineage of the Idea The framing isn't new — only the courage to take it seriously is. Richard Dawkins coined meme in 1976 to name the gene's quieter, faster cousin: a unit of culture that replicates through imitation. Daniel Dennett and Susan Blackmore extended the logic to words themselves, calling them the most successful mind-viruses on Earth. Terrence Deacon traced the deeper spiral: language and brain co-evolved, each rewiring the other across millennia. The parasite lens is what happens when you stop being polite about what those findings imply.

03 — Hidden WiringBricks Made of Breath

Pick a word. Follow it outward.

Time demands clocks, calendars, shifts, deadlines, the global timekeeping infrastructure that costs billions a year to maintain. Property demands fences, deeds, registries, courtrooms, a class of humans paid to shoot other humans over a line in a document. Sin demands confessionals, guilt, denominations, inquisitions, the architecture of shame. Money — a word so contagious it now lives inside computers as pure ledger — demands banks, ATMs, algorithms trading it at the speed of light, entire lives bent toward its accumulation.

The pattern holds across every domain: a word enters, the brain reshapes, the hand builds. Cities are word-storage devices. Highways are sentences made of asphalt. The internet is a dictionary that learned to breathe.

We poured the concrete so the words would have somewhere to live.

Once you see it, you can't unsee it. The boss is a word wearing a suit. The mortgage is a sentence with thirty years of interest. The border is a comma between two populations that the word nation decided were different species. None of these things existed before the words did. The words came first. Then they taught us to forge their furniture.

04 — CounterweightDon't Burn the Dictionary

The lens is a lens, not a verdict.

Language also lets a mother sing her child to sleep. It lets a surgeon say tumor and a poet say almost and a friend say I'm here. The cooperative magic is real; the cognitive scientist Michael Tomasello has spent a career documenting how shared intentionality made us, not diminished us. To call language purely a parasite is to flatten a symbiosis into an infection.

We are hosts, yes — but also gardeners. Some guests we invited. Some we would die without. Some we should never have let in the door, and they're still in the kitchen at 2 a.m., eating our future.

The honest claim is narrower than the provocation: language is not neutral. It does not merely describe reality; it insists on a reality, and then commissions us to build it. Knowing that changes the question. The question stops being "How do I use language?" and becomes "Which words am I being used by — and do I consent?"

05 — AgencyBite Back, Softly

The parasite is not in control. You are — but only when you remember you're being spoken.

Small, concrete moves. Not a manifesto. A toolbox.

  • Notice the demand. When productivity makes you feel guilty for lying in the grass, ask: whose word is that? What does that word need me to do — and who profits when I obey?
  • Retire a word. Some words stop earning their keep. Should is a common one. Try a week without it. Watch the obligations evaporate.
  • Refuse a word's claim. Being "late" without the word late is just walking. Try it once, on something small. Notice that nothing collapses.
  • Name the guest. When failure shows up at 2 a.m., address it by name. "You're a word. You're old. Sit down." Words lose their grip when you stop mistaking them for weather.

The point isn't to dismantle language. The point is to stop being furniture inside your own house.

06 — The MoveName Your Cage

In the next twenty-four hours, do exactly this.

The 24-Hour Move

Find the word. Ask the question. Find the door.

Write down — on paper, on your phone, on the back of a receipt — one word that has been running your life. A word that decides how you spend an hour, a dollar, or a Tuesday. Productivity. Success. Respect. Busy. Behind. Enough. Pick the one that bites.

Then ask the only question worth asking of a parasite you've chosen to keep:

What did I build today only because this word told me to?
  • If the answer is something you wanted to build — keep the word. Feed it. It's earning its rent.
  • If the answer is something you didn't want — you've just found the door. You don't have to walk through it tonight. But you know where it is now.

That's it. One word. One question. One door. The parasite doesn't die from a single act of refusal. But it does flinch. And you'll feel the flinch — which is how you'll know, for the first time in a long time, that the voice in your head was never quite yours.
