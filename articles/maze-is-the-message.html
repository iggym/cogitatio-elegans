The Maze Is the Message

Automated support wasn't built to help you. It was built to outlast you.

Technology · July 2025 ·
Perspective Lens
24/7 Access & Efficiency

Automated support chatbots and interactive voice menus provide round-the-clock access to problem resolution. They reduce wait times, route inquiries efficiently, and deliver service at scale—freeing human agents for complex cases while ensuring no customer is turned away after business hours.

Attrition by Design

These systems function primarily as psychological friction walls designed to exhaust consumer persistence, effectively weaponizing user frustration to suppress complaints, returns, and support costs. The loop isn't a glitch. The maze is the point.

Left on Read

It's 2:47 PM on a Tuesday. Margaret has been on hold for forty-three minutes. Before that, she spent eleven minutes navigating a phone tree that asked her to "say or press" her way through seven branches, none of which described her problem. The recorded voice—warm, professional, almost human—keeps thanking her for her patience.

She has been disconnected twice. Each time, she called back. Each time, she started from the beginning. The same menu. The same options. The same hold music, wrapping around itself like a Möbius strip of synthetic reassurance.

Margaret is not unusual. She is not unlucky. She is the design.

Every year, consumers spend an estimated 1.2 billion hours on hold. That number is not an accident. It is an output—a predictable result of systems engineered to produce exactly this experience: a long, exhausting, demoralizing wait that makes giving up feel like the rational choice.

Smoke and Mirrors

Here is the claim that changes everything about how you see that phone tree, that chatbot, that endlessly looping support portal: these systems function primarily as psychological friction walls designed to exhaust consumer persistence, effectively weaponizing user frustration to suppress complaints, returns, and support costs.

The wait is not a bug. The loop is not a glitch. The maze is the point.

When a company deploys an "AI-powered assistant" that cycles you through three rounds of "I understand your frustration" before offering a link you already tried, it is not failing to help you. It is succeeding at something else entirely. It is converting your complaint into a data point about "customer engagement." It is absorbing your energy, resetting your urgency, and quietly, efficiently, reducing the probability that you will ever reach a human being empowered to fix your problem.

The system does not need to solve your issue. It needs to make solving your issue more expensive—in time, in patience, in emotional currency—than living with the problem itself.

Tangled Web

Consider what connects: the phone tree that never quite has the right option. The chatbot that responds to your detailed complaint with a list of FAQ links. The callback feature that's always "unavailable." The satisfaction survey that arrives before your problem is resolved. The "press 1 for urgent" option that routes you to the same hold queue as pressing 3.

These are not separate failures. They are interconnected components of a single system—an attrition architecture. Customer-facing automation technologies often act as administrative buffers engineered to deflect corporate accountability. Each layer adds friction. Each loop reduces the population of complainants. Each "I didn't quite catch that" is a small, deliberate erosion of your will to continue.

The brilliance of the design is its deniability. No single step is obviously hostile. A phone menu is just organization. A chatbot is just efficiency. A hold queue is just high call volume. Only when you step back and trace the full path—the entire funnel from complaint to surrender—does the architecture become visible.

Flip Side

To be fair, not every automated system is a trap. Some genuinely route you faster. Some chatbots resolve simple queries in seconds. A 24/7 support portal does provide access outside business hours. The technology itself is neutral—what matters is the incentive structure behind it.

When a company measures support success by "cost per contact" rather than "first-contact resolution," the system's design follows the metric. When executives are rewarded for reducing call center headcount, the "AI-powered assistant" that cycles most callers back to the website isn't a failure—it's performing exactly as intended.

The nuance matters. Not every phone tree is a weapon. But every phone tree is shaped by the question: "How do we reduce the cost of hearing from you?" And the answer to that question, more often than not, is: "Make it harder for you to reach us."

Fighting Chance

But here's what the attrition wall depends on: your silence. Every person who gives up after the third transfer is a data point confirming the system works. Every complaint abandoned mid-chatbot is a cost saved. The architecture only holds if you stop pushing.

The countermeasure is stubborn, specific, and small. Document everything. When the chatbot fails, screenshot the transcript. When the phone tree disconnects you, note the time and the branch. When the support portal logs you out after twenty minutes of filling in forms, save your text before you hit submit.

File complaints with your state's consumer protection office. These agencies track patterns, and patterns force regulatory attention. Use the company's own channels against them: post the transcript on social media, reference the specific timestamp of your disconnection. The system is designed to absorb individual complaints. It is not designed to absorb documented, public, patterned evidence.

One screenshot won't bring down a wall. But a thousand screenshots, filed in a thousand complaints, aggregated into a pattern that a regulator can't ignore—that changes the math. The wall wins when walking away costs less than pushing through. Make pushing through cost less. Make the wall expensive to maintain.

Your Move

Within the next 24 hours, find one automated system that's wearing you down. Don't abandon the interaction. Document it. Then file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau or your state attorney general's office. The wall wins when you walk away. Make them count the cost of keeping you out.
