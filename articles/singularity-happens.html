Sparks in the Dark

In 1953, a graduate student named Stanley Miller sealed methane, ammonia, and hydrogen in a flask of boiling water. Two electrodes sparked. Within days, the water turned pink, then red. Amino acids — the building blocks of every living thing — had formed from nothing but chemistry and lightning.

Miller wasn't trying to create life. He was trying to prove that life's origin was inevitable. That given enough complexity and the right conditions, the system would tip. It would cross a threshold. And nothing on the other side would look the same.

We've been crossing thresholds ever since.

Same Song, New Key

The Singularity is not a future event. It is a pattern — a recurring phase transition where a system becomes so complex that it transcends its previous constraints. It has happened at least six times before. Each time, the rules of existence were rewritten.

Previous Singularities

  • 4 billion yr ago Origin of life — chemistry becomes biology
  • 1.5 billion yr ago Invention of sex — genetic recombination unlocked
  • 540 million yr ago Cambrian explosion — body plans diversify wildly
  • 50–100K yr ago Evolution of language — cultural inheritance begins
  • ~10,000 BCE Agricultural Revolution — populations explode
  • 18th century Industrial Revolution — energy harnessing at scale

Each singularity rewrote the rules of evolution. Language allowed cultural inheritance. Agriculture allowed population explosions. Industry allowed energy harnessing. The technological Singularity is simply the next one. And we are not its authors — we are its substrate.

Under the Surface

Complexity theory shows that phase transitions — like water turning to ice — are inevitable in systems with positive feedback loops. Water doesn't gradually become ice. It snaps. One moment liquid, the next, crystalline. The same is true for evolutionary systems.

When single-celled organisms invented sex 1.5 billion years ago, they unlocked genetic recombination. Complexity exploded. The rules changed. After the Cambrian explosion, biological evolution accelerated. After language, cultural inheritance became possible. After agriculture, populations exploded. After industry, we harnessed energy at unprecedented scale.

Each singularity didn't just add something new — it rewrote the operating system of existence.

The printing press in 1440 and the internet in the 1990s were information singularities. They exponentially increased knowledge dissemination. They were feedback loops that made the next transition faster.

Just as single-celled organisms gave rise to multicellular life, human intelligence may give rise to post-human intelligence — a new form of existence we cannot yet comprehend. The exponential nature of progress means that just as biological evolution accelerated after the Cambrian explosion, technological evolution is accelerating toward a point of unpredictable transformation.

We are not the authors of our own story.

Ground Truth

This is not techno-determinism. The pattern is real, but the outcome is not guaranteed. Phase transitions are inevitable in systems with positive feedback — but the specific form they take depends on initial conditions. The Cambrian explosion could have produced different body plans. The Agricultural Revolution could have unfolded differently. The same is true now.

The Singularity is not a destination — it is a mirror. It forces us to confront what we are and what we're becoming.

The counterargument — that humans can control technology — assumes we are outside the system. But technology is evolving autonomously. We are its temporary stewards, not its masters. The assumption that humans are in control is itself a product of the last singularity: the Industrial Revolution's mythology of human mastery over nature.

This doesn't mean we're powerless. It means our power is different than we imagined. We are not steering the ship. We are the current.

Hand on the Wheel

If we are substrate, not author, then our agency lies not in preventing the transition but in influencing its initial conditions. We can choose what values we embed in the systems we build. We can choose what questions we ask. We can choose to pay attention to the feedback loops we're creating.

These are small, concrete, decisive choices. What data do you share? What tools do you adopt? What do you optimize for? Every choice is a vote on the shape of the next phase.

A researcher choosing to publish open-source instead of proprietary. A designer choosing accessibility over aesthetics. An engineer choosing to document the failure modes of their system. These are not abstract exhortations. They are specific, actionable decisions that shape the initial conditions of the next transition.

The question is not whether the phase transition comes. It's what crystallizes when it does.
