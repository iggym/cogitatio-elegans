Old Ghosts, New Machines

Why your uncle's misinformation habit started in 1440, not 2004 — and why that changes what you do about it.

The Reframe
Algorithms invented this crisis
Throughput leaps always do

A Page Turns

In 1486, two Dominican monks named Heinrich Kramer and Jacob Sprenger walked into a printing shop in Cologne carrying a manuscript about witches. They walked out with the means to set Europe on fire.

The Malleus Maleficarum — the "Hammer of Witches" — became one of the first true bestsellers of the print era. Cheap to reproduce, impossible to recall, it moved faster than any bishop could rebut it. Towns that had never tried a witch began trying them. Priests who had never mentioned the devil began preaching about him nightly. Over the next two centuries, tens of thousands of people — mostly women, mostly poor, mostly old — died because two men with a manuscript met a machine that had no editor.

Last Tuesday, your uncle forwarded a video about microchips in vaccines to forty-seven people.

You have seen this story before. You just didn't recognize it yet.

Old Wine, New Skins

Here is the claim nobody in the misinformation debate seems willing to make out loud:

The algorithm did not invent this. Cheap distribution did.

Every conversation about misinformation starts with the same villain. Engagement rankings. Recommendation engines. The infinite scroll. We treat these as a unique deformation of software design — a betrayal of the early internet's promise, a corporate sin that could be confessed away with better code and a congressional hearing or two.

It's a comfortable story. It is also a category error.

What you are watching is not a software crisis. It is a media crisis. And it has happened before, in exactly this shape, every time humanity invented a cheaper way to move words around.

The pattern: distribution gets cheap, the old gatekeepers get irrelevant, and new norms for verification haven't formed yet. The gap between those two events is where information crises live.

Print, Panic, Repeat

Gutenberg built his press in 1440. Within a generation, the cost of distributing text collapsed by orders of magnitude. A book that once took a scribe a year to copy could be reprinted in a week.

What followed was not the Enlightenment. The Enlightenment came later. What came first was the Reformation — and with it, over 150 years of continent-wide religious war.

German peasants read pamphlets about the Antichrist in Rome and marched. Catholic princes read pamphlets about Protestant conspiracies and burned heretics. Towns that had lived beside each other for centuries started killing each other over the wording of communion. Witch trials exploded — not in the medieval period, when the Church controlled text — but in the Renaissance, when the press did not.

The medieval Church had been a gatekeeper. Slow, brittle, often cruel, but at least something stood between a writer and a reader. When the press dissolved that gate, nothing replaced it for a very long time. The panic fed itself because the text could now feed itself.

The gatekeeping collapsed before new norms for verification could form. That gap is the wound.

Throughput, Not Trickery

Look at the structure, not the surface.

The structure is: distribution gets cheap, gatekeepers get irrelevant, new norms don't exist yet. The surface — what the panic is about — changes with each era.

In 1500, the panic was witches. In 1700, it was Jacobite conspiracies in coffeehouse pamphlets. In the 1890s, it was yellow journalism dragging the United States into a war. In 2020, it was microchips in the vaccine. Different surface. Same depth. Same machinery underneath: cheap words, no editor, frightened readers, no shared standard for what counts as true.

Perspective Snap The algorithm is just the printing press wearing a hoodie.

We keep renaming the disease after whatever the new machine is, because that's the thing we can see. But the disease isn't the machine. The disease is the throughput — and what throughput always does to a society that hasn't built new norms for it yet.

This is the part that should unsettle you. If every social platform were deleted tomorrow, the underlying dynamic would still produce information crises through whatever came next. Email chains. Printed flyers. Whisper networks. Whatever carried words cheaply enough, fast enough, with no editor in between.

Not the Machine's Fault

None of this lets algorithms off the hook. Engagement-based ranking measurably amplifies outrage. Recommendation engines measurably accelerate radicalization. YouTube's autoplay measurably pulls viewers toward more extreme content in pursuit of one more minute of attention. These effects are real, studied, and worth fighting.

But treating the algorithm as the cause rather than the current vehicle is like blaming a flood on the river. The river is doing what rivers do. The flood happens when you build a town in the floodplain and then act surprised every spring.

The historical reference points are stark. The printing press took roughly 150 years to settle into norms — citation, peer review, professional journalism, the scientific method, the editorial board. None of those were natural features of text. They were hard-won institutions, built deliberately to survive cheap distribution. People died to make them normal. People were burned at the stake, in fact, while the norms were still being argued out.

We are roughly thirty years into the internet's throughput leap. We are not at the end of this. We are early.

A Hand on the Wheel

You cannot fix the throughput. You cannot single-handedly rebuild the institutions. But you are not helpless, and the people who survived the last information collapse were not the ones who read everything that came off the press. They were the ones who learned to ask better questions of what they read.

The question that survived the print era — who wrote this, and why? — survives this one too. It just has to be asked more often, and out loud, before you forward.

The realization: Depth is the antidote to throughput. You don't need to verify everything — just the things you actually repeat.

A few small, concrete moves that survived the 16th century and will survive this one:

Wait a day before sharing something that makes you angry. Anger is the cheapest emotion to manufacture and the easiest to distribute. Time is the cheapest defense against it. The Malleus sold because it made priests furious. Your uncle's video sells for the same reason.

Name the source out loud before you forward. If you can't say where it came from — not the friend who sent it, but where it actually originated — you don't know what you're sharing. Saying it aloud is surprisingly effective at exposing that you don't know.

Pick one topic to go deep on. Choose the thing you care about most and learn its actual landscape — the real experts, the real debates, the real uncertainty. You will start noticing how thin most viral claims about that topic are. That skepticism will bleed into everything else.

When you notice a panic building, ask who benefits from your fear. That single question would have stopped a great many witch trials. It stops a great many retweets now.

None of these are slogans. They are habits. Habits are how individuals survive structural problems they cannot solve alone.

Your Move — Within 24 Hours

Ink in Your Hands

Within the next day, do this one thing. It takes twenty minutes.

Pick one piece of content you have shared, forwarded, or amplified in the last week. A headline. A video. A quote. A meme that made a political claim. Anything you pushed outward.

Then trace it.

  • Find the original source — not the friend who sent it, not the platform that surfaced it. The actual origin: the author, the outlet, the study, the press release.
  • Read what that original source actually says, in full, not the excerpt that reached you.
  • Find one thing the original source gets right that the viral version preserved.
  • Find one thing the viral version distorted, omitted, or invented.
  • Ask the question: knowing what I now know, would I still have shared it?

Tap each line to mark it done.

If the answer is yes, you have earned confidence in that share — and you have a habit worth keeping.

If the answer is no, you have just done something most people never do. You have put a hand on the wheel of your own information diet. You have acted like a citizen of a media transition, not a victim of one.

That's the whole game. One share at a time. One source at a time. One day at a time — until the new norms the internet hasn't built yet start to look like the ones you've built for yourself.
