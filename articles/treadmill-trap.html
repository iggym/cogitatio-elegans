The Treadmill Trap

We like to think we’re climbing a ladder of progress. But what if we’re just sprinting on a treadmill, running faster and faster just to avoid falling off?

Ladder of Progress Treadmill of Dependency
The shift from "solving problems" to "managing the solutions"

Outrunning Our Own Shadow

It’s 2:00 AM. The hospital hallway hums with sterile, fluorescent light. Dr. Aris stares at a petri dish under her microscope. The bacteria that should have died are thriving. A decade ago, this antibiotic was a miracle. Today, it’s barely a speed bump. She reaches for the phone to order the next experimental drug, knowing full well that in two years, the bacteria will adapt to that, too.

She isn’t curing the disease. She’s just keeping it at bay.

We like to think of human ingenuity as a ladder. You invent the car, you climb a rung. You invent the internet, you climb another. But look closely at Dr. Aris’s petri dish. That isn’t a ladder. It’s a treadmill. Every step forward simply sets the stage for a new problem that demands an even faster sprint.

The Treadmill Trap

We assume technology makes our lives easier. It is the defining myth of the modern age: that the next innovation will finally give us breathing room.

Technology doesn't make life easier. It makes it more complex. Every innovation creates new vulnerabilities that demand even more innovation to manage, trapping us in an escalating cycle of dependency.

We aren’t advancing toward a frictionless utopia. We are running faster and faster just to stay in the exact same place.

Up the Down Escalator

To understand why this happens, we have to look outside our own species. In 1973, evolutionary biologist Leigh Van Valen coined the Red Queen Hypothesis. He noticed that species don’t evolve in isolation. Gazelles run faster to escape cheetahs; cheetahs run faster to catch gazelles. Neither animal gets ahead. They just get faster, locked in an endless biological arms race just to survive.

Technology operates on the exact same logic. Take Moore’s Law. Since 1965, computing power has doubled roughly every 18 to 24 months. But what has that actually done? More power means we generate more data. More data demands more storage. More storage creates bigger targets for hackers, demanding complex cybersecurity. You don’t buy a faster computer to do less. You buy it to manage the crushing weight of your previous computers.

We saw this on a geopolitical scale during the Cold War (1947–1991). Each side built a better bomb, forcing the other to build a bigger one. It didn’t make anyone safer. It just trapped both superpowers in mutually assured destruction (MAD)—a global treadmill where slowing down meant annihilation.

We aren't climbing a ladder. We're sprinting on a treadmill.

Mirrors and Mutations

This doesn’t mean innovation is inherently evil. Antibiotics did save millions of lives. The internet did connect the globe. But the illusion is that these tools eliminate struggle. They don’t. They relocate it.

We traded bacterial infections for antibiotic resistance. We traded geographical isolation for cybersecurity threats and the existential risk of AI alignment. We traded the slow march of natural climate change for the rapid destabilization caused by industrial emissions. Every solution is a new problem in disguise.

The question is no longer whether we can keep up with our own creations—because we have to. The real question is whether the treadmill will eventually collapse beneath us under the sheer weight of our dependencies.

Stepping Off the Belt

How do you step off a moving treadmill without flying off the back? You don’t have to throw away your phone and live in the woods. Meaningful change comes from choosing which races you participate in.

You don’t have to adopt every innovation blindly. When a new tool promises to save you time, ask what new complexity it introduces. When a new system promises to automate your life, ask what new dependency it creates. Agency isn't about stopping the world; it's about auditing your inputs. You can choose to run a different race—one where the finish line isn't just "more."

One Pause, Today

You don't need to dismantle your life. You just need to break the cycle of automatic adoption for one moment.

  • Pick one app or automated tool that promised to make your life easier but now feels like a chore to manage.
  • Turn it off completely for 24 hours.
  • Do the task manually. Notice if you actually miss the complexity, or if you just rediscovered a bit of solid ground.
