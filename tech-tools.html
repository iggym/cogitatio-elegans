Technology & The Human Condition

The Tool Doesn't Care Who Holds It

Every technology in history has been a referendum on who gets to decide. Once you see that axis, you can't unsee it — and you can start using it to choose your tools instead of being chosen by them.

Rented — power moves away from you Owned — power stays with you

This is the only spectrum that matters for judging a new tool. The rest of this piece is about how to read it.

I

The Phone in the Field

In a village outside Kisumu, a farmer named Otieno stands in his maize field holding a phone that cost him less than a week's wages. He isn't checking social media. He's checking the price of maize in three different markets, forty kilometers apart, before he decides which one to walk to.

Six years ago, that decision belonged to the middleman who drove the only truck through the village — a man who quoted whatever price he liked, because Otieno had no way to know better. Now Otieno knows better. The middleman still comes. But he no longer sets the price alone.

That's the whole story, and it's also not the whole story. Because forty kilometers in the other direction, in a different village, a different farmer holds an identical phone — and the app on it siphons his location, his yield data, and his purchase history back to a company that uses it to predict his desperation and price its seed loans accordingly.

Same device. Same signal bars. Opposite outcome.

II

The Question the Chart Skips

This is the moment we keep skipping past when we talk about technology and human progress: the device is never the story. The device is inert. What decides whether a phone liberates a farmer or quietly farms him is a single variable nobody puts on the marketing slide — who holds the decision rights once the tool is in the room.

Here is the claim, stated plainly, because it deserves to be stated plainly:

The Thesis

Technology does not improve the human condition by existing. It improves the human condition only when it returns decision-making power to the individual holding it — and it degrades the human condition, often while looking like progress, when it quietly moves that power somewhere else.

This is not the story we're usually told. The usual story is a straight line: innovation happens, adoption follows, life gets better, chart goes up and to the right. That story isn't false so much as it's incomplete in exactly the place that matters. It measures the tool. It never asks who's holding the leash.

III

The Hidden Axis

Run this test against anything you'd call a "technology," and the pattern holds with uncomfortable consistency. The printing press was progress because it broke the Church and the Crown's monopoly on interpreting scripture and law — it moved a decision (what do I believe, what do I know) down to the individual reader. The radio, a century later, did the opposite: it took a village's worth of independent conversations and replaced them with one voice, broadcast to millions, deciding what millions would think about at the same hour.

Same category — "communication technology." Opposite direction of power. One built agency. The other borrowed it and didn't give it back.

Once you see the axis — centralizing versus decentralizing decision rights — you start noticing it's the hidden wiring beneath ideas that seemed unrelated. Civilizations don't collapse primarily because armies arrive. They erode for decades first, as the tools ordinary people once used to feed themselves, store their wealth, and settle their disputes get quietly absorbed into the hands of the few who can administer them at scale.

Money follows the identical pattern, and it's the clearest version of it, because money is really just a technology for storing the decisions you've already made — the hours you worked, the risks you took — so you can spend them later. A currency that can be debased at will isn't a neutral tool anymore; it's a tool that quietly transfers your stored decisions to whoever controls the printing press, one percentage point at a time, without ever needing your consent.

Energy runs the same wiring. A grid you don't control, priced on someone else's schedule, is a dependency dressed as an amenity. A solar panel on your own roof — however small, however inefficient compared to the grid — is a decision right, physically returned to you.

Every civilization that lost its freedom didn't lose it to armies first — it lost it to the tools it had already let someone else control.

Once that clicks, you stop asking "is this new technology impressive?" and start asking the only question that ever actually mattered: after I adopt this, who has more say over my life — me, or the entity that built it?

IV

Where the Line Breaks

I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the kind of framework that's easy to overstate into a slogan, and slogans lie by omission. Centralization is not always theft, and decentralization is not always liberation. A century of centralized public health infrastructure eradicated smallpox — a decision no individual household could have made alone, and the species is better for it. Meanwhile, plenty of "decentralized" technologies have simply created a thousand small tyrannies instead of one large one; a farmer freed from the middleman's price can still be trapped by a moneylender, an algorithm, or his own panic.

The Honest Version

Not "decentralized good, centralized bad" — the danger isn't concentration itself, it's concentration without a corresponding ability to absorb shocks locally. A system where every decision routes through one node isn't dangerous because centralization is evil; it's dangerous because when that node fails, or turns predatory, there is no fallback.

Distributed decision-making isn't morally superior. It's structurally more resilient, because the failure of one part doesn't collapse the whole. Fragility tends to concentrate quietly, and resilience tends to require that ordinary people retain at least one lever they don't have to ask permission to pull.

V

The Lever You Already Have

None of this is abstract, and none of it requires you to overhaul your life. It requires you to notice, this week, which levers you've quietly handed away — and to take back exactly one.

The farmer in Kisumu didn't need a revolution. He needed one piece of information he used to lack, and the will to walk the extra kilometers once he had it. That's the whole shape of agency, almost every time it shows up in real life: not a grand gesture, but a small, decisive refusal to stay dependent on a decision someone else was making for you by default.

You already have candidates for this in your own life, and you know exactly what they are, because you feel the friction every time you think about them. The subscription that holds your only copy of something irreplaceable. The platform that decides what you see before you decide what you want to see. The savings sitting somewhere that quietly lose purchasing power every year while looking, on the statement, exactly the same. The skill you keep outsourcing to a tool instead of learning the ten percent of it that would let you catch the tool being wrong.

Each of those is a small technology — a system someone else built and you adopted — and each one is currently set to either return you a decision right or quietly absorb one. You don't have to fix all of them. You have to notice which one costs you the most, and take it back first.

VI

Twenty-Four Hours

So here is the twenty-four-hour version of all of this, stripped down to something you can actually do before tomorrow ends: pick one thing in your life you currently rent — access to your own money, your own data, your own information, your own attention — and convert it, even partially, into something you own and control outright.

Before Tomorrow Ends

Take back one decision right.

It doesn't need to be dramatic. It needs to be physically returned to your own hands. A few honest starting points — pick the one with the most friction, not the easiest one:

  • Export the file you only have one copy of
  • Move money out of a quietly depreciating account
  • Cancel the feed that decides what you see
  • Learn the 10% of a skill you've outsourced
  • Back up what you can't afford to lose

That's the whole test technology has always had to pass, one tool and one person at a time. Not "does it work." Not "is it impressive." Just this: after I use it, do I have more say over my own life, or less?

Answer that question honestly today, for one thing, and you'll have done more for the human condition than a thousand words about it ever could.
